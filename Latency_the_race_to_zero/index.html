★

TL;DR — the whole talk in 12 lines

  • Latency is money in fintech: the fastest market makers quote the tightest spreads. What matters is your order's latency, not the system's average — and predictable (tail) latency beats a good mean.
  • History repeats: relay systems (Roman cursus publicus, Pony Express) cut latency by splitting the journey into stages. The Pony Express cut Missouri→California from 3–5 weeks to 10 days… and went bankrupt 18 months later when the telegraph arrived.
  • Hardware no longer saves us: CPUs have more cache layers and shared memory, cloud adds abstraction layers — throughput keeps rising but latency doesn't. Communication between components is now the bottleneck.
  • The Disruptor's real win (2010) was not the ring buffer — it was separation of concerns: journaling, decoding and business logic each get their own thread, never waiting on each other.
  • Aeron (2014): efficient reliable UDP unicast/multicast + IPC messaging. Echo benchmark: ~31 µs in cloud (Java ≈ C!), ~12.6 µs with DPDK kernel bypass, ~6.6 µs on-prem with ef_vi, ~0.6 µs over IPC shared memory.
  • Better than IPC? A function call — a virtual call is tens of nanoseconds; an inlined call is "really zero". The dream: a distributed system where you choose the communication channel per link, down to function calls when components co-locate.
  • The academic backbone: replicated state machines (deterministic: events in → state → events out) + a totally ordered log (Virtual Synchrony, 1987) give you replication, checkpoints, built-in time, and "fastest wins" delivery for free.
  • Fault tolerance needs quorum consensus (Paxos → Raft). Aeron Cluster implements Raft; its strong leader gives predictable latency. Consensus round-trip: ~84 µs cloud Java, ~15.7 µs P50 on-prem ef_vi, P999 < 29 µs.
  • But that cluster design has 3 problems: fan-out (1 order → many output events over one egress), scale (limited by that egress), recovery (upgrading a state machine disturbs the cluster).
  • The sequencer fix (Island ECN, 1996 → NASDAQ): the cluster decides only the log — ordering + timestamps. State machines move into the applications. Output events never cross the wire → no fan-out, cheap recovery, elastic scale.
  • Conclusion: a virtually synchronous distributed system with state machine composition — where a "message" between co-located machines can literally be a function call — is how we get closer to zero.

01

Why latency matters — and what "latency" means here

It's directly linked to profit 01:30

In fintech, latency translates straight into money. At Amir's previous company the market makers with the lowest latency quoted the smallest spreads — they could afford to, because they could get in faster and move their prices faster than everyone else when the market changed.

Average latency is the wrong metric

"If I send an order to a trading system, I really don't care what the average latency of that system is. I only care about the latency of my order." That's why this talk always reports percentiles (P50 / P99 / P999) — see every table below.

Low latency also matters in recovery: while a system recovers it is unresponsive, and "unresponsive is unavailable". Minimizing recovery time is a latency problem too.

What "latency" means in this talk 02:30

The time to complete one operation end-to-end in a mission-critical distributed system (e.g. a trading system). Such a system must also be scalable, resilient, and must never lose a message — and most of that time is spent communicating between components, not computing.

Agenda & speaker 03:05

02

The Past — latency reduction is an old sport

The talk opens with three "famous examples from the past where latency was reduced", each with a lesson for software people.

🏛️ Roman Empire: cursus publicus 05:00

Rome's famous roads were only part of it: a whole system of horses, wagons, riders and relay stations along the way, where officials could swap the horse, the wagon or the rider. Official messages and goods moved at far lower latency than private traffic. Speed was "good for the Empire" since Augustus, ~2,000 years ago.

⚡ The electrical telegraph (1830s–40s) 05:20

An invention directly born from latency: Samuel Morse was away on a business trip when his wife died unexpectedly; the news didn't reach him in time. Devastated, he set out to build something so future generations would get the message in time.

🐴 Pony Express (1860–61) — a great success that went bankrupt 06:02

A relay of horses and riders with stations along the route cut message delivery from Missouri to California from 3–5 weeks to 10 days. Two things make it interesting:

  1. Nothing was invented — relay staging was known for more than a thousand years (Rome!).
  2. The company went bankrupt in 1861, just 18 months after launch — because the telegraph reached the US west coast, and a fundamentally better technology wiped out the incremental one.
Lesson for engineers: pure incremental optimization (better horses, more stations) loses to a technology that changes the game (electrons instead of horses). Keep that in mind when we get to kernel-bypass messaging and sequencers.
03

The Present — why hardware can't help anymore

Until about two decades ago, the software industry had a trivially easy latency solution: buy new hardware. That free lunch is over. "The world is changing!" is the section title on the slides — and the changes all point the same way:

“The most amazing achievement of the computer software industry is its continuing cancellation of the steady and staggering gains made by the computer hardware industry.” — Henry Petroski, quoted on the slide 07:35

What developers naively do about it 10:00

So the real question, which the rest of the talk answers 11:40:

Can we design a low-latency distributed system without compromising scalability, resilience and predictability — and what are the trade-offs?
04

2010 — the LMAX Disruptor: separation of concerns

In 2010 LMAX open-sourced the Disruptor, a very efficient way of passing objects between threads in Java — "a very efficient queue, really". It used every trick in the book:

  • No memory allocation on the hot path (events are pre-allocated in the ring).
  • Exact understanding of memory barriers and what the system does with them.
  • A ring buffer at the heart — "something hardware designs knew from the 1970s. Again, nothing was invented here."
The tricks helped, but the one big thing that gave the huge latency boost was separation of concerns — a way to separate the work streams of the system: journaling of an incoming message was decoupled from decoding it, which was decoupled from the business logic. Each thread does exactly one thing, is never interrupted, and never waits for anything. That's where the real latency improvement came from.

The Disruptor in code (official getting-started example)

From the official Disruptor user guide (Apache 2.0). This is what "a very efficient queue between threads" looks like in practice — note the pre-allocated events and the power-of-2 ring size.

// Event objects are pre-allocated by the factory — no allocation on the hot path
public class LongEvent {
    private long value;
    public void set(long value) { this.value = value; }
}

int bufferSize = 1024;  // must be a power of 2

Disruptor<LongEvent> disruptor =
    new Disruptor<>(LongEvent::new, bufferSize, DaemonThreadFactory.INSTANCE,
        ProducerType.SINGLE, new BlockingWaitStrategy());

disruptor.handleEventsWith((event, sequence, endOfBatch) ->
    System.out.println("Event: " + event));   // the business-logic stage
disruptor.start();

RingBuffer<LongEvent> ringBuffer = disruptor.getRingBuffer();
ByteBuffer bb = ByteBuffer.allocate(8);
for (long l = 0; true; l++) {
    bb.putLong(0, l);
    ringBuffer.publishEvent((event, sequence, buffer) -> event.set(buffer.getLong(0)), bb);
}
Real-world impact: the Disruptor came out of the LMAX exchange's retail trading platform, which processed 6 million TPS on a single thread (as reported in the famous 2011 Disruptor paper & InfoQ article "Disruptor: High performance alternative to bounded queues…"). It later influenced parts of the Log4j2 async logger and other frameworks. Paper: Disruptor-1.0.pdf.

2014 — Aeron: transport latency numbers, decoded

In 2014 Martin Thompson and Todd Montgomery open-sourced Aeron: an efficient, reliable, low-latency message transport for UDP unicast, UDP multicast and IPC (inter-process communication via shared memory) between processes.

"Cloud" below means GCP (other providers differ, "but the actual numbers don't matter so much — it's more the difference"). The speaker's own lab hardware is called on-prem / PerfLab.

How to read this table — the four takeaways

  1. Java ≈ C. "What do you think — higher or lower? It's both: about the same." The JIT does the heavy lifting; Java is not slow if you keep allocation off the hot path (see Q&A §10). 16:40
  2. Kernel bypass is huge in the cloud. DPDK lets Aeron skip the whole OS network stack — "bypass all those layers of abstraction, including the operating system, including the sockets" — and write Ethernet packets straight to the NIC: 31 µs → 12.6 µs.
  3. Own hardware beats cloud, and ef_vi beats DPDK on it. On the PerfLab rig, Aeron integrates with ef_vi (Solarflare/Xilinx Onload's kernel-bypass layer) to reach single-digit microseconds.
  4. IPC ≈ 0.6 µs — but both processes must be on the same host to share the memory. That's the trade-off.
What are DPDK and ef_vi? DPDK (Data Plane Development Kit) is a set of userspace poll-mode NIC drivers: your process owns the NIC and no kernel interrupt/socket layer is crossed. ef_vi is the low-level kernel-bypass interface shipped with Solarflare/Xilinx (OpenOnload) — more NIC-specific, but even leaner. Downside (from the Q&A): they're tightly bound to specific NICs; swap your hardware and you may need a different integration, or have no support at all.

Better than IPC? A function call. 18:50

  • A virtual function call: tens of nanoseconds (i.e. ~0.02–0.05 µs — still ~20× faster than IPC's 0.6 µs).
  • A call the compiler inlines: "this really is zero".
So the goal becomes: design a distributed system with separation of concerns, where we control the communication channels — send over the network when we must, but use a function call whenever two components are worth co-locating. That idea returns in §6 and §9.

Aeron in code (official BasicPublisher / BasicSubscriber samples)

From the official aeron-samples in the Aeron repository (Apache 2.0), lightly abridged. A channel + stream ID identify a message stream; aeron:udp?endpoint=… for network, aeron:ipc for shared-memory IPC.

// --- Publisher (BasicPublisher.java) ---
try (Aeron aeron = Aeron.connect(ctx);
     Publication publication = aeron.addPublication(CHANNEL, STREAM_ID))
{
    final UnsafeBuffer buffer = new UnsafeBuffer(BufferUtil.allocateDirectAligned(256, 64));

    final int length = buffer.putStringWithoutLengthAscii(0, "Hello World! " + i);
    final long position = publication.offer(buffer, 0, length);

    if (position > 0)                 { /* sent */ }
    else if (position == Publication.BACK_PRESSURED) { /* retry / slow down */ }
    else if (position == Publication.NOT_CONNECTED) { /* no subscriber yet */ }
}

// --- Subscriber (BasicSubscriber.java) ---
try (Aeron aeron = Aeron.connect(ctx);

{
    final FragmentHandler fragmentHandler = SamplesUtil.printAsciiMessage(STREAM_ID);
    SamplesUtil.subscriberLoop(fragmentHandler, FRAGMENT_COUNT_LIMIT, running).accept(subscription);
}
06

The theory backbone — replicated state machines & the log

"Nothing is invented here" — to build the system above, Amir goes back to decades of distributed-systems research:

The replicated state machine (RSM)

Deceptively simple, and that's its power:

  • Events in → the machine deterministically modifies its state → events out.
  • It knows only events; it is fully asynchronous — no calls, no waiting.
  • Determinism is the contract. Example from the talk: model a matching engine — orders come in, the state is the order book, execution reports go out.

Virtual Synchrony ingredient 1: the totally ordered log 23:15

  • Replication for free: feed the same totally-ordered sequence of messages to several deterministic state machines, and they all compute exactly the same state — that is replication.
  • Checkpoints: a state machine can announce "I've processed message 2, not yet 3" — every component knows exactly where every other component is.
  • Time for free: the timestamps in the log are the system's clock — no extra clock synchronization, no clock-drift worries.

Ingredient 2: logical groups 25:00

Put together: all replicas in a group have exactly the same state at the same checkpoint. From a latency standpoint, that buys you four things:

  • …and with multiple actives, mean-time-to-recovery of an instance is effectively zero: when a crashed instance comes back, the others already produced its messages.

State machine composition — the function-call trick 29:02

  1. Dependency composition: two state machines consume the same log; at the same checkpoint it is safe for one to query the other synchronously (e.g. Matching Engine asks the Markets reference-data machine "get market").
  2. Pipeline composition: machine A's output events are machine B's input events (e.g. Matching Engine → Market Data Aggregate).
This is the answer to "better than IPC": compose state machines inside one process, and their communication is a function call — while the system still behaves like one distributed, replicated whole.

The second separation: business logic is decoupled from infrastructure. State machines don't care how the log is persisted or distributed — they only see events in, events out, and their state.

07

Fault tolerance — quorum, Raft, and Aeron Cluster

A log on one machine is a single point of failure. Distributed-systems research's answer: run the log on a quorum of 3/5/7 nodes running a consensus protocol.

Why Raft? The Aeron open-source cluster implements the Raft consensus protocol (Ongaro & Ousterhout, 2013 — designed to be understandable, "a raft from the island of Paxos"). Raft has a strong leader: the leader isn't rotated around; it's strong until it dies. That gives more predictable latency.

  • Why no IPC row? The whole point of consensus is fault tolerance via independent machines. Everything on one host shares its fate — "we don't do consensus over IPC."
08

The system this gives you — and its 3 problems

Problem 1 — fan-out

In fintech, one message in typically produces many messages out. All of those output events squeeze through the single egress channel — just managing that volume becomes the bottleneck.

Problem 2 — scale

Because everything condenses into the egress, you can't just add more state machines and more data; the egress caps the system.

Problem 3 — recovery / upgrades

Upgrading a state machine means taking a cluster node down — which costs the consensus protocol — or running hot standbys or other workarounds.

09

The Future — the Sequencer architecture

The sequencer pattern has been in fintech for decades: the first company to talk about it publicly was Island ECN in 1996, and their sequencer ended up as the sequencer in NASDAQ.

Island ECN was founded in 1996 (Josh Levine / Datek), one of the first electronic communication networks for US equities, and grew to handle a large share of NASDAQ volume; via Instinet/INET its technology — including its sequencer-based matching system — was absorbed into NASDAQ. (Background: Wikipedia — Island ECN.)

The idea: the cluster decides only the log

Why this solves all three problems of §8:

  • No fan-out problem: the cluster no longer emits output events — it publishes the log, "a condensed version of all the input events". Output events stay inside the applications where they're produced.
  • Better scale: many groups of composed state machines can consume the same log — scale out on the consumer side.
  • Cheap recovery: upgrading a state machine = restarting an application; it comes back and resumes consuming the log from its last checkpoint. The cluster never notices.

Putting it all together — a full trading system 39:18

Conclusion 40:11

Status check (from the Q&A): the sequencer is a future product — "it doesn't exist yet, it's only on our laptops, or a very crude version". That's why no benchmark was shown: an echo test would look like the cluster numbers; the sequencer's power only shows in real distributed scenarios, which is the intention. It is being developed with Martin Thompson and others at Adaptive as Aeron Sequencer.
10

Q&A highlights 40:40

Q: What are the downsides of kernel bypass (DPDK / ef_vi)?

It's very low level and tightly tied to the network interface card. Replace your hardware and you may need a different integration — or that NIC may have no support at all.

Q: Why use Java at all if IPC is ~10 µs but loading a class takes 50 µs?

"Java is fast, really." The JIT's optimizations change the game — the fact that Java and C reach the same numbers in the tables proves it. Caveats: don't "allocate memory like mad" or the GC will destroy your predictable latency; pre-allocate, keep the hot path allocation-free. "This fight has ended — Java is not slow by any means."

Q: If one component is slow, does the sequencer stall the whole system?

Business dependencies you cannot remove — "if that's the business problem, that's the business problem." What you can remove is artificial coupling: decouple, separate concerns, work asynchronously with queues — send the message, do other work, react when the response arrives.

Q: What from low-latency engineering should go mainstream / into the cloud?

"The short answer is I don't know." Low-latency reliable message protocols could be pushed lower into cloud stacks, but the cloud has its own challenges (availability zones, unknown hardware underneath) — "doing low latency in the cloud is tough". Customers who care enough still buy their own hardware; maybe cloud providers will one day offer customized low-latency reliable messaging.

Glossary

P50 / P99 / P999
Median / 99th / 99.9th percentile latency. P999 means 1 in 1000 operations is slower. Tail percentiles are what "predictable latency" is judged by.
Kernel bypass
Networking where the application talks (almost) directly to the NIC, skipping the OS socket stack — via DPDK or ef_vi. Big latency win; couples you to specific hardware.
DPDK
Data Plane Development Kit — userspace poll-mode NIC drivers for packet processing.
ef_vi
Low-level kernel-bypass interface from Solarflare/Xilinx (the layer under OpenOnload); NIC-specific.
IPC
Inter-process communication — here: Aeron passing messages through shared memory between processes on the same host.
Disruptor
LMAX's open-source (2010) inter-thread messaging library: a pre-allocated ring buffer with sequence barriers; famous for separation-of-concerns design.
Aeron
Open-source (2014) efficient reliable UDP unicast/multicast + IPC message transport, by Martin Thompson & Todd Montgomery.
Replicated state machine (RSM)
Deterministic unit: input events → state change → output events. Feed identical input sequences to replicas and they stay identical.
Totally ordered log
The single agreed sequence (seq + timestamp) of all input events — the backbone of Virtual Synchrony.
Checkpoint
A state machine's position in the log ("processed message N") — enables recovery, safe queries between co-located machines, and coordination.
Virtual Synchrony
Birman et al., 1987: the model combining a totally ordered log + logical groups with replicated membership.
Quorum / consensus
3/5/7 nodes agree on the log so it survives node failures (Paxos, Viewstamped Replication, Raft).
Raft
Understandable consensus protocol (Ongaro & Ousterhout 2013) with a strong leader — the basis of Aeron Cluster. Strong leader ⇒ predictable latency.
Sequencer
Component that only orders and timestamps the log (no business logic); state machines live in the applications. Island ECN 1996 → NASDAQ; future "Aeron Sequencer".
MTTR
Mean time to recovery. With multiple active deterministic replicas it is ~0: survivors already produced the missing outputs.
